Dubai's food and beverage market is enormous, competitive and unforgiving, and the ownership of its restaurants turns over constantly: concepts that worked are sold at their peak, concepts that did not are sold on the way down, and the listing portals carry both at asking prices that tell you little about either. Selling a restaurant well here is absolutely possible, but it follows rules of its own, because a restaurant is a different asset from a trading company: its value lives in a lease, a licence, a location and a stream of daily covers, all of which a buyer can verify or destroy faster than in any other sector. This guide covers what your restaurant is actually worth, what moves that number, and how the sale mechanically works.

## Two markets, not one

Understand which market you are selling into, because they price differently:

- **Going-concern sales.** A profitable restaurant sold as a working business: revenue, staff, brand, lease and licence together. These are scarce on the portals, attract operators and groups rather than dreamers, and command real multiples of profit.
- **Asset and licence sales.** A venue sold for its fit-out, equipment, licence and location, with the trading history mattering little or not at all. This is the larger half of the visible market, sometimes called key-money deals, and the price is driven by what a new concept would otherwise spend to open in that spot.

Most owners believe they are in the first market. The numbers decide, and buyers run them without sentiment: a restaurant that does not reliably make money is an asset sale, whatever the listing says.

## What a restaurant is actually worth

For a profitable, owner-run venue, the base is the same arithmetic as any small business, applied strictly: normalised owner earnings times a multiple, and restaurant multiples sit at the **lower end** of the SME ranges in [how much is my business worth](/insights/how-much-is-my-business-worth-uae/), because buyers price the sector's risks: trends move, leases end, and margins live at the mercy of rent, food costs and staffing. A venue with several years of stable, verifiable profit, a long lease and a concept that does not depend on its founder can beat those ranges; a one-location concept two years old cannot, however busy Friday brunch looks.

For loss-making venues, the value is the alternative cost of the site: fit-out in usable condition, equipment, the licence, and the lease terms. Two honest warnings here. The **fit-out fallacy**: what you spent on the interior is not what it is worth, because used fit-out transfers at a deep discount and tastes differ; three million dirhams invested commonly supports a fraction of that in a sale. And equipment resells for little; its value in a sale is that the buyer avoids procurement time, not the invoice price.

One sector advantage worth using: restaurants produce **verifiable revenue** like almost nothing else, through POS data, delivery-platform statements and supplier invoices. A seller whose declared numbers match the POS export is believed, and priced, accordingly.

## What buyers pay more for

- **Lease length and terms.** The single biggest value lever. A venue with two years left on the lease is worth dramatically less than the same venue with seven, and a sale usually needs the landlord's consent, which makes the landlord a party to your deal. Renew before you market, not during diligence.
- **Clean, consistent POS history.** Cash leakage is endemic in the sector, and it destroys value twice: the skimmed revenue is invisible to the buyer, and the habit undermines trust in everything else.
- **A healthy delivery mix.** Delivery revenue with sane platform economics extends the catchment; delivery dependence with thin margins is a weakness dressed as growth. Buyers look at the split and the net margins per channel.
- **Independence from the founder or chef.** Documented recipes, trained kitchen leadership and SOPs make the concept transferable. If the food is one person, the buyer prices the risk that the person leaves with the sale.
- **Compliance without blemish.** Food-safety record, municipality inspections, staff files and visas in order. In a licensed-venue sale, permits and approvals that transfer cleanly carry real money.
- **Franchise potential.** A concept with documented operations and a replicable format sells a future, and some buyers pay for it; a beloved one-off sells a present.

## Preparing the sale: the six-month version

The full preparation logic is in [preparing a company for sale](/insights/prepare-company-for-sale-timeline/); the restaurant-specific sprint looks like this. Run six to twelve clean months through the POS with personal spending separated out, because those months are the evidence everything rests on. Fix the lease first, everything else second. Document the kitchen: recipes, costings, supplier terms, opening and closing procedures. Stabilise the team you want the buyer to inherit, quietly. And assemble the file a serious buyer will request on day one: licence and permits, lease, POS exports, delivery statements, staff list with visa status, equipment list.

## The process, and where it differs

The sale itself follows the standard confidential arc in [how to sell a company in the UAE](/insights/how-to-sell-a-company-in-dubai-uae/), with three sector pressure points. **Confidentiality is fragile**: staff in hospitality move at rumours, suppliers talk, and the landlord hearing about a sale from someone else starts the consent conversation badly, so disclosure is sequenced carefully and [NDAs](/insights/confidentiality-when-selling-a-business-nda/) come before numbers. **The buyer pool needs filtering**: alongside genuine operators and groups, restaurant listings attract first-time buyers in love with the idea of owning a restaurant, and unfiltered they consume months; proof of funds early is not rudeness, it is process. **Completion runs through the authorities**: licence transfer, municipality and food-permit changes, staff visa transfers and the landlord's formal consent, which together set the closing timetable more than the contract does.

## Selling a struggling restaurant honestly

The most common case deserves straight talk. If the venue loses money, the realistic routes are an asset-and-licence sale at the site's alternative value, a going-concern sale priced for the turnaround the buyer must fund, or a negotiated exit from the lease where even key money is unrealistic. What rarely works is spending further savings to dress the venue up for a buyer who will read the POS history anyway; the numbers are the product, and six months of genuine improvement beats any cosmetic refresh. If speed matters because cash is running out, the discipline in [selling a business fast](/insights/sell-my-business-fast-uae/) applies, including its hardest rule: the deadline is never disclosed.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much can I sell my restaurant for in Dubai?

A profitable venue: a multiple of normalised owner earnings, at the cautious end of SME ranges, lifted by a long lease, verifiable POS history and independence from you. A loss-making venue: the value of the fit-out, equipment, licence and location to the next concept, which is usually far below what was invested. The POS data decides which conversation you are in.

### How do I sell my restaurant fast?

Have the file ready before approaching anyone, price off the real numbers, approach operators already running venues (they decide fastest), and secure the landlord's position early, because their consent is the usual long pole. Distressed timelines tempt owners to skip the NDA discipline; resist that, the staff finding out early costs more than the days saved.

### Can I sell a restaurant that is losing money?

Yes, as an asset and licence sale: the buyer pays for the site, fit-out, equipment and permits rather than the trading history. The price reflects what opening fresh in that location would cost them. If the losses are recent and explainable, a going-concern sale priced for recovery is sometimes achievable, but it must be argued from numbers, not atmosphere.

### Do I need the landlord's permission to sell my restaurant?

In practice yes, almost always: either the lease transfers to the buyer, which needs the landlord's consent, or a new lease is signed, which needs the landlord entirely. Engage them early, understand what they will ask for, and treat their consent as a condition of the deal in the paperwork.

### What documents do buyers ask for?

POS exports and delivery-platform statements, the lease, trade licence and food permits, supplier contracts, the staff list with visa status, equipment and fit-out schedules, and financial statements reconciling to the POS. A seller who produces these within days is taken seriously; one who cannot is priced as a risk.

## The takeaway

A restaurant sells on four things a buyer can check: the numbers in the POS, the years on the lease, the permits that transfer, and whether the concept survives your departure. Fix those and Dubai's deep pool of operators and investors is reachable; ignore them and the market prices your venue as a site.
