Owners searching for business valuation services in the UAE are usually solving one of a handful of very different problems: a sale on the horizon, a partner to buy out, a dispute heading to court, an auditor asking for numbers, or an investor across the table. The valuation industry serves all of these, but with different products, different providers and very different price tags, and buying the wrong product for your situation wastes both money and time. This guide sorts it out: what each situation actually requires, who provides it in the UAE, and how to choose.

## The situations that call for a valuation

- **Selling the company, or raising capital.** You need to know what the market would pay before anyone negotiates with you. The methods are the classic ones described in our [valuation methods guide](/insights/business-valuation-methods-uae/), applied with current transaction evidence.
- **A partner buyout or a stake transaction.** Two parties with opposite interests need one number they both accept; an independent valuation converts an argument into a calculation, as we describe in the [partner buyout guide](/insights/partner-buyout-uae/).
- **Disputes, arbitration and court.** Shareholder conflicts, divorce, inheritance. Here the valuation is evidence, so it must be a formal expert product that survives cross-examination.
- **Audit and financial reporting.** Purchase price allocations, impairment testing, fair-value measurements. Accounting-driven work with prescribed standards, usually commissioned by the CFO rather than the owner.
- **Tax and regulatory events.** Restructurings, transfers between related parties, and corporate-tax positions increasingly need supportable values behind them; see [UAE corporate tax and M&A](/insights/uae-corporate-tax-and-ma/).
- **Planning.** Succession, insurance, employee incentive schemes, or simply an owner who wants to know what the life's work is worth. Lighter requirements, where over-buying is common.

## Two different products

Almost everything above resolves into one of two products, and the distinction matters more than any brand name.

A **formal valuation report** is a documented, standards-based opinion: methodology set out, assumptions stated, signed by a named valuer, built to be defended before a court, an auditor or a tax authority. It is the right product for disputes, financial reporting and regulatory needs, and it is priced accordingly, because most of the cost is the documentation and the liability behind the signature.

A **market view** answers a different question: what would credible buyers actually pay for this business today, and who are they. It is built from the same methods plus live transaction evidence, moves faster, costs less, and is the right product when the purpose is a sale, a raise or a negotiation. Our guide to [how much your business is worth](/insights/how-much-is-my-business-worth-uae/) shows the arithmetic that underpins it.

The classic mistake runs in both directions: paying for a courtroom-grade report to set an asking price (over-engineered, and often anchored to theory rather than buyers), or walking into litigation with an advisor's one-page estimate (which the other side's expert will take apart). Match the product to the purpose and half the selection problem disappears.

## Who provides valuation services in the UAE

- **Audit and accounting firms**, from the global networks to mid-tier practices. The natural home for financial-reporting and tax-driven valuations, and for formal reports where an internationally recognised name carries weight.
- **M&A advisory firms.** Valuations grounded in what buyers and investors are actually paying, because the same firms run the transactions. The natural choice when the valuation precedes a sale, a raise or a buyout negotiation, and often folded into the wider mandate.
- **Specialist valuation practices and chartered valuers.** Focused shops, often with credentials such as RICS or CFA-charterholder teams, covering everything from SME valuations to expert-witness work.
- **Court-appointed experts.** In UAE litigation the court often appoints its own expert; parties then need their valuations prepared to a standard that stands up in that process.
- **Banks and lenders** value businesses and assets for collateral purposes. Useful to understand, and not a substitute for any of the above, because collateral values are deliberately conservative.

## What it costs, honestly

Prices vary too widely for a reliable menu, but the drivers are consistent: the **purpose** (a defensible expert report costs a multiple of a market view), the **state of your records** (clean, audited numbers cut the work dramatically), the **complexity** (one operating company versus a group with related-party dealings), and the **deadline**. A simple market view for an SME sits at the affordable end; a contested-litigation expert report for a multi-entity group sits at the other, with most needs falling between. Two practical notes: where the valuation precedes a sale mandate, advisory firms often absorb much of it into the engagement, and the cheapest quote is frequently the most expensive outcome, because an unusable valuation gets paid for twice.

## How to choose a provider

Five questions filter the market quickly:

1. **Is the product matched to the purpose?** Ask directly whether they would recommend a formal report or a market view for your situation, and why. A provider who sells one product for every purpose is answering a different question than yours.
2. **What transaction evidence do they hold in your sector?** Multiples from a database are a starting point; deals the firm has actually seen priced are better.
3. **Who signs, and with what standing?** For anything that may face a court or auditor, the individual's credentials and track record as an expert matter more than the firm's logo.
4. **Is the fee independent of the outcome?** A valuer paid more for a higher number is not a valuer. Success-based fees belong to the transaction mandate, never to the valuation itself.
5. **What do they need from you, and how long will it take?** A provider who asks for nothing before quoting has not understood the job.

## Frequently asked questions

### Who can value a business in the UAE?

There is no single licence that monopolises business valuation, so the market includes accounting firms, M&A advisory firms, specialist valuers and court experts. What matters is matching the provider to the purpose: standards-based reports for courts, auditors and tax; transaction-grounded market views for sales, raises and buyouts.

### How much does a business valuation cost in the UAE?

It depends on purpose, record quality and complexity more than on company size. A market view for a straightforward SME is the least expensive product; formal expert reports for disputes or financial reporting cost several times more. Ask for a fixed quote against a defined scope, and be suspicious of quotes offered before anyone has looked at your numbers.

### What is the difference between a valuation report and a market valuation?

A formal report is a documented, signed opinion built to defined standards, for use before courts, auditors and authorities. A market valuation answers what buyers would pay today, using live transaction evidence, and is the working tool for sales and negotiations. They can differ in number as well as form, because they answer different questions.

### How long does a business valuation take?

A market view on a prepared company typically takes two to four weeks. Formal reports run longer, commonly four to eight weeks, driven mostly by how quickly information arrives and how complex the group is. Litigation timetables follow the court's clock, not the valuer's.

### Do I need a valuation before selling my business?

You need a market view before anyone negotiates with you; walking into a sale without one means discovering your price from the buyer. A formal standards-based report is usually unnecessary for a sale itself, though specific situations (regulated sectors, related-party steps in the structure) can require one alongside.
