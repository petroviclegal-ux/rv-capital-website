Every owner exits. The only open questions are when, on whose terms, and at what price. Owners with an exit strategy answer those questions themselves, years in advance; owners without one have the answers handed to them by health, markets, partners or fatigue, at the worst possible negotiating moment. An exit strategy is unglamorous work: a destination, a route and a preparation schedule written down while nothing is urgent. This guide covers the five routes that actually exist, what being exit-ready means in practice, and the schedule that turns a someday into a transaction.

## The five real exit routes

- **A trade sale.** Selling the whole company to a strategic or financial buyer. The route that usually pays the most, because a competitive process can be run around it, and the default destination for most owners; the mechanics are in [how to sell a company in the UAE](/insights/how-to-sell-a-company-in-dubai-uae/).
- **A partial exit.** Selling a stake, taking money off the table, and continuing with a partner or investor. Fits owners who want de-risking before departure, or a bigger second bite later; the stake mechanics are in [selling or buying shares in a UAE company](/insights/selling-or-buying-shares-in-a-uae-company/).
- **A management buyout.** The team you built buys the company, usually financed over time. Quieter and often faster than a market process, at a price that reflects the absence of competition; see [how MBOs work in the UAE](/insights/management-buyout-mbo-uae/).
- **Family succession.** A transfer of management, ownership or both to the next generation. The route with the longest honest timeline, because successors need years, and the one where governance (who decides what, documented) matters most; many of the considerations in [selling a family business in the GCC](/insights/selling-a-family-business-gcc/) apply even when nothing is sold.
- **An orderly wind-down.** Collecting the receivables, selling the assets, settling the obligations, closing well. The honest route for businesses that are really a practice built around one person and will not survive a transfer. Choosing it deliberately, early, beats discovering it late after a failed sale process.

Most owners end up combining routes: a stake sale now, the remainder to the same buyer in three years; an MBO for the operating company and a trade sale for the property. The strategy is picking the primary route and keeping a second one alive as negotiating gravity.

## What exit-ready actually means

Whatever the route, the same company travels best: one that **runs without its owner**, with a manager or team the buyer can keep; **clean, audited numbers** with personal expenses long separated out; **revenue on contracts** rather than relationships held in the founder's phone; **customers spread** so no single loss changes the story; and **tidy legal foundations**, from the licence and shareholder documents to the lease. These are the same factors that set the multiple in [how much is my business worth](/insights/how-much-is-my-business-worth-uae/), and the deeper point is that they compound before any exit: an exit-ready company is simply a better company to own, every year you still own it. Preparation is not a cost of selling; it is value creation that happens to also enable selling.

## The three-year schedule

A year is the working minimum for sale preparation, as our [12-month timeline](/insights/prepare-company-for-sale-timeline/) sets out. Three years is the honest number for a full exit strategy, because the changes that move multiples are slow:

- **Three years out:** choose the primary route and the target range. Fix the structural issues: recruit or promote the manager who reduces your centrality, start the audit relationship, begin spreading concentrated revenue.
- **Two years out:** run the company visibly well under the new structure, so the track record exists by the time a buyer examines it. Move key relationships onto contracts. Resolve the quiet legal and tax untidiness while it is cheap; with corporate tax now in force, structure questions belong here, not in the deal (see [UAE corporate tax and M&A](/insights/uae-corporate-tax-and-ma/)).
- **One year out:** valuation, advisor, process. From here the standard sale timeline takes over, and everything done in the two years prior shows up directly in the offers.

The schedule also keeps options open in the other direction: owners who complete it and then decide not to sell have lost nothing, and own a stronger company.

## The exit section of a business plan

Investors read the exit strategy section of a business plan for one thing: how they get their money back. If you are raising capital, that section should name the realistic route (trade sale to the consolidators in your sector, a buyback arrangement, a listing only where that is genuinely plausible), the horizon, and the comparable transactions that make it credible; vagueness here reads as not having thought about the investor at all, a point we expand in [how to find an investor](/insights/find-an-investor-for-your-business-uae/). For your own planning the section is simpler still: the route, the number that makes you leave, and the triggers (age, offer level, market conditions) that would start the process early.

## The mistakes that force bad exits

- **Waiting for the perfect moment.** The forced alternatives (health, burnout, a partner's exit, a downturn) arrive unscheduled; the [signs it is the right time to sell](/insights/signs-right-time-to-sell-your-business/) are worth reading while they are still optional.
- **Negotiating with the single buyer who showed up.** An exit strategy exists precisely so an [unsolicited approach](/insights/someone-wants-to-buy-my-business/) becomes the start of a process rather than the whole process.
- **Pricing the dream instead of the numbers.** The market pays multiples of verified profit, with no sentiment line in the model.
- **Telling no one until it is urgent.** Exits need prepared successors, informed advisors and orderly records; secrecy until the deadline produces none of them. Confidentiality and preparation are not the same thing.
- **Leaving tax and structure for the deal table.** Reorganising ownership mid-transaction costs time, leverage, and sometimes the deal.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a business exit strategy?

A written plan for how and when you will convert ownership of your company into money or a managed succession: the chosen route (sale, partial sale, MBO, family transfer or wind-down), the preparation the business needs to travel well, and the triggers that would start the process. Its purpose is to make the exit a decision rather than an event.

### What is the best exit strategy for a small business?

For most owner-run SMEs, a trade sale after one to three years of deliberate preparation pays the most, with an MBO or partial sale as the alternatives where confidentiality or continuity matter more than the last dirham. For businesses inseparable from their owner, an honest early choice between building transferability and planning an orderly wind-down beats defaulting into a failed sale later.

### When should I start planning my exit?

Three years before you want the option, which usually means now. The expensive value drivers (owner independence, audited numbers, spread revenue) take years to build, and all of them make the company better to own in the meantime.

### What should the exit strategy section of a business plan say?

For investors: the realistic route by which they get their capital back, the horizon, and the evidence that such exits happen in your sector. For yourself: the primary route, the number, and the triggers that would start the process earlier than planned.

### Can I exit without selling to a stranger?

Yes: a management buyout, a family succession, or a partial sale to an investor while you step back gradually. Each trades some price for continuity and control over the handover, and each still requires the same preparation, because the company must survive your departure under any of them.

## The takeaway

An exit strategy is the difference between leaving your company and losing control of how you leave it: route chosen, business made transferable, schedule running quietly in the background while you keep building. Start three years before you want the option, and the option is real; start when you need it, and someone else sets the terms.
